General Relativity

Raytracing through curved spacetime, relativistic hydrodynamics, and gravitational-wave inspiral — light bending, black hole shadows, accretion, and tidal disruption.

General relativity work here comes at the same physics from three angles: raytracing null geodesics through curved spacetime to render what a black hole actually looks like, evolving relativistic fluid on a fixed black-hole background to simulate accretion and tidal disruption, and integrating a post-Newtonian binary inspiral to produce a gravitational-wave chirp. The raytracers and the inspiral model are Python; the hydrodynamics solver is a C++/OpenGL code from a shared simulation framework built for real-time, GPU-friendly physics.

Light bending & black hole shadows

A raytracer through the Schwarzschild spacetime of a static black hole bends camera rays along null geodesics instead of straight lines, rendering gravitational lensing, the photon sphere, and a lensed accretion disk.

Left: the lensed accretion disk seen from further back, showing the full disk wrapping around the shadow with a secondary lensed image arcing above it. Right: a swooping camera trajectory — varying both distance and viewing angle, rather than a fixed orbit — reveals how the photon ring and disk lensing change as the camera moves.

Extending the same raytracer to a spinning (Kerr) black hole adds frame dragging: the black hole’s shadow shrinks and shifts off-center as spin increases, and a disk co-rotating with the hole (prograde) can orbit much closer in than one orbiting against it (retrograde), since frame dragging moves the innermost stable orbit itself.

Left: the shadow shrinking and shifting as spin a goes from 0 to near-extremal 0.998. Right: at a=0.9, the prograde disk's inner edge (r_isco=2.32M) sits much closer to the hole than the retrograde disk's (r_isco=8.72M).
The raytraced light-bending angle validated against the analytic strong-deflection limit across impact parameters.

Relativistic hydrodynamics & tidal disruption

A general-relativistic hydrodynamics solver evolves fluid on a fixed Kerr background using a conservative, shock-capturing finite-volume scheme. Below, a Fishbone-Moncrief torus settles into a steady accretion flow around a spinning (a=0.9M) black hole.

The same simulation framework, run in a Newtonian limit with a Paczynski-Wiita pseudo-potential to approximate strong-field gravity cheaply, models a star being tidally shredded on a close encounter with a black hole (impact parameter beta=3):

Left: the cheap pseudo-potential closely tracks a full relativistic treatment through pericenter passage. Right: the classic t^(-5/3) fallback-rate signature of tidal disruption events, measured directly from the disrupted debris.

Gravitational waves

A two-body system integrated under Newtonian gravity plus leading-order radiation reaction turns a fixed ellipse into an inspiral, draining orbital energy into gravitational waves. Run with GW150914’s published parameters (m1=36, m2=29 solar masses, distance 410 Mpc), it reproduces the real event’s characteristic chirp — rising amplitude and frequency as the binary spirals in. The simulated orbital decay rate validated against the Peters (1964) closed-form formula for gravitational-wave-driven inspiral.

The simulation stops at r=6M — a conservative cutoff past which leading-order post-Newtonian gravity is no longer trustworthy, well before the true strong-field merger (which needs full numerical relativity). The inspiral chirp leading up to that point is the physically meaningful part of this simulation; the final frequency reached here undershoots GW150914’s real measured peak of 150-250 Hz accordingly.
